Doubling spend doubles output and nothing more. Reaching the estimated 10 million hours costs $0.5–1.4 billion, and every hour is paid for again: there is no economy of scale in collection.

Why cost does not fall with scale
Capacity is added by adding rigs and operators. Every increment of capacity is new equipment and new labour.
Single-operator output is capped. <200 demonstrations a day: 1,000 rigs at full utilization yield about 2 million hours a year, a fifth of the requirement.
No learning curve. Collection is capital-intensive operations, not software; the ten-millionth hour costs what the first did.
Collection must be targeted: the algorithm decides what to collect, and every hour must become a reusable, provable asset.

Performance in a new environment is set by the number of environment–object pairs covered, not by hours at one site. Collection budget therefore goes to new pairs, and the model’s failures decide which pairs come next.

Structural gaps of the three participant types

Our view
Embodied data must be amplified and refined by algorithms. Ground first (map trajectories to structures that support reasoning), then verify (per-record consistency), then augment (physically consistent simulation for scarce cases).
Amplifiers and refiners create value only inside a loop. Each stage’s output must feed the next and pay back the previous; otherwise the algorithm is one more middleware layer.
This requires a party with prior accumulation. The kernel, the verification methods and a measured-data foundation take years to build (pp.14–19, p.40).
