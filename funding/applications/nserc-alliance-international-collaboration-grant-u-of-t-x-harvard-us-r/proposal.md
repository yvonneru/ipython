## 1. Relevance and expected outcomes

An ontology is a computer-interpretable specification that declares what terms a system uses and what they mean. My research axiomatizes ontologies as first-order theories and verifies them: we characterize the models of an ontology up to isomorphism and determine whether they are the intended models. This methodology produced COLORE, an open repository of 2,580 first-order ontologies, the Process Specification Language (PSL), and the upper ontology TUpper, published as ISO/IEC 21838-4:2023 [1]. Dr. Yi Ru, a core contributor to that standard and now a postdoctoral researcher at Harvard Medical School in [the international collaborator's group], developed with me a theory of material constitution as a parthood-preserving mapping between mereologies and a validation of mereological pluralism [2, 3]: verified first-order theories that state exactly when two part decompositions of the same object are compatible.

That question is now an engineering problem. Robot-learning datasets — PartNet, PartNet-Mobility (SAPIEN), GAPartNet, [PartNet-Ensembled] and the real-robot corpora [AgiBot World], Open X-Embodiment and DROID [4–9] — annotate objects as hierarchies of parts with kinematic, functional and visual labels. Each defines "part" operationally: a segmentation label in one, a movable link in another, an actionable region in a third. No dataset or learned model is required to satisfy the axioms of parthood; there is no principled way to map labels across datasets; pooled training silently mixes incompatible vocabularies. The consequences — poor transfer across part vocabularies, brittle generalization to new object categories — are observed but unmeasured, because there has been no formal specification to measure against. My Discovery Grant program undertakes the ontological analysis of vision benchmarks, asking which mereotopologies the decompositions of ShapeNet and PartNet presuppose; the present project starts from that analysis and does what it does not: it extends to articulated and real-robot corpora, automates the audit at corpus scale, uses the ontology to train policies, and joins the international project described in §2. The long-term challenge is:

*Can the part-level representations that robot-learning systems learn be shown to satisfy the axioms of parthood that humans use, and does enforcing those axioms improve generalization?*

The Canadian component has three objectives.

1. **Ontology as specification.** Axiomatize and verify a modular first-order ontology of physical object parts — rigid, articulated, functional and assembly parts — as an extension of TUpper integrated with PSL for state change under manipulation. Verification (consistency, non-triviality, module relationships, and representation theorems relating the ontology to each dataset's annotation schema) uses Prover9 and Mace4 within the COLORE methodology [10], so that cross-dataset label mappings are meaning-preserving by proof.
2. **Ontology as audit.** A two-tier pipeline — Datalog/SMT rules for millions of part instances, first-order theorem proving for the residual hard cases — translates annotation hierarchies into ontology instances and checks them. Output: the first quantitative measurement of parthood consistency in robot datasets, corrected annotations, proved label mappings, a merged ontology-aligned corpus, and a reusable audit toolkit, which the international team applies to [its annotation domain] to test generality.
3. **Ontology as inductive bias.** Training methods that use the ontology to shape part-aware manipulation policies: differentiable relaxations of parthood constraints as auxiliary losses; constraint-guided augmentation of ontology-consistent part configurations for underrepresented categories; and verification-in-the-loop evaluation that reports a policy's ontology-violation rate alongside task success. Hypotheses: ontology-consistent training improves generalization to unseen PartNet-Mobility categories; violation rate predicts task failure; pooled ontology-aligned data transfers across part vocabularies. Simulation in SAPIEN [and a second simulator] in Toronto; [real-robot validation on the international team's platform, if option A in §2].

**Milestones.** Year 1: verified parts ontology released under an open licence and submitted to COLORE; representation theorems for five annotation schemas; audit pipeline and first audit of PartNet, PartNet-Mobility and GAPartNet. Year 2: audit extended to the real-robot corpora; public release of corrected annotations, mappings and the merged corpus; pooled-data and augmentation experiments; audit of the international team's domain. Year 3: constraint-based training and verification-in-the-loop evaluation; [real-robot] validation; contribution of the parts module to ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42; two machine-learning papers, one robotics paper, one applied-ontology paper.

**Benefit to Canada.** The verified ontology, audit toolkit and aligned corpus are open infrastructure built and maintained in Canada, in the laboratory that developed COLORE and its verification tooling. They give Canadian robotics and manufacturing users a machine-checkable statement of what their training data means — evidence of the kind the EU AI Act will require of AI in machinery and robots from 2 August 2028 [11]. The project keeps Canada's contribution to ISO/IEC 21838 current as physical AI becomes its main application, and brings a University of Toronto-trained researcher back from a US group to train Canadian students.
