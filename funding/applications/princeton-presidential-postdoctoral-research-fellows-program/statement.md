## Contributions to the academic community — Yi Ru

The academic community I have contributed to is the one that forms around a research problem when people from different fields, career stages and institutions are given the tools to work on it together. My contributions have been of three kinds: representing graduate students, teaching and mentoring in engineering, and building open standards and open tools that let people outside a laboratory use what the laboratory has produced. I describe each, and then what I would do at Princeton.

**Representing graduate students.** At the University of Toronto I served as Vice-President of the graduate students' association of the Department of Mechanical and Industrial Engineering (AMIGAS), as the department's representative to the University of Toronto Graduate Student Union, and as a steward and representative for CUPE 3902, the union of teaching assistants and sessional instructors. The three roles are different kinds of work: the association organized the seminars, orientation and social life that turn a department into a community; the student-union seat carried departmental concerns to the university level; the stewardship meant sitting with individual teaching assistants who had a grievance and helping them through a process most were facing for the first time. **Teaching and mentoring.** I have been a teaching assistant in the Faculty of Applied Science and Engineering for four courses — Engineering Economics and Accounting; Database Systems; Knowledge Modeling and Management; and Resource and Production Modeling — and I have supervised or mentored [number] undergraduate and master's project students [confirm]. Outside the university, as a founding team member of MICAS I led a team of more than one hundred people, most of them early in their careers, and as a co-founder of YourTable Inc. I worked with students through incubation programs at the University of Toronto, the Schulich School of Business and Imperial College London. Leading a team is not mentoring, but it teaches the same thing at scale: people do their best work when the standard is explicit, the feedback is specific and the credit is honest. I am bilingual in English and Mandarin Chinese [and have presented this work to academic and industry audiences in North America and Asia — confirm]; part of what I bring to any group is the ability to make a formal subject accessible to people who did not train in it.

**Open standards and open tools.** *(optional)* As a core contributor to ISO/IEC 21838-4:2023 [confirm role wording and working-group designation], I have done research inside an international standards committee, ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42. Standards work is community work of an unglamorous kind: the result is a document that thousands of implementers will use and few will cite, produced by consensus among national bodies over years. It is the most direct way I know of making formal methods available to people who are not logicians. The same commitment governs the proposed program: every axiom, mapping, audit result, corrected annotation and line of code will be released under an open licence and contributed to the COLORE repository, so that groups without access to proprietary robot data or to a verification laboratory can build on the work.
