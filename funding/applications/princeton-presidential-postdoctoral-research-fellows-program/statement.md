## Statement on contributions to the academic community — Yi Ru

The academic community I have contributed to is the one that forms around a research problem when people from different fields, career stages and institutions are given the tools to work on it together. My contributions have been of three kinds: representing and organizing graduate students, teaching and mentoring in engineering, and building open standards and open tools that let people outside a laboratory use what the laboratory has produced. I describe each, and then what I would do at Princeton.

**Representing graduate students.** At the University of Toronto I served as Vice-President of the graduate students' association of the Department of Mechanical and Industrial Engineering (AMIGAS), as the department's representative to the University of Toronto Graduate Student Union, and as a steward and representative for CUPE 3902, the union of teaching assistants and sessional instructors. The three roles are different kinds of work. The association organized the seminars, orientation and social life that turn a department of several hundred graduate students into a community; the student-union seat carried departmental concerns to the university level; the stewardship meant sitting with individual teaching assistants who had a grievance and helping them through a process that most were facing for the first time. **Teaching and mentoring.** I have been a teaching assistant in the Faculty of Applied Science and Engineering for four courses — Engineering Economics and Accounting; Database Systems; Knowledge Modeling and Management; and Resource and Production Modeling — spanning first-year and graduate students, and I have supervised or mentored [number] undergraduate and master's project students [confirm]. Outside the university, as a founding team member of MICAS I led a team of more than one hundred people, most of them early in their careers, and as a co-founder of YourTable Inc. and Uing Technologies I worked with students from the University of Toronto, the Schulich School of Business and Imperial College London through their incubation programs. Leading a team is not mentoring, but it teaches the same thing at scale: people do their best work when the standard is explicit, the feedback is specific and the credit is honest. I am bilingual in English and Mandarin Chinese and have presented this work to academic and industry audiences in North America and Asia; part of what I bring to any group is the ability to make a formal subject accessible to people who did not train in it.

**Open standards and open tools.** *(optional)* Much of my research has been carried out inside ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42, the international standards committee for artificial intelligence, where I was a core contributor to ISO/IEC 21838-4:2023 [confirm role wording and working-group designation]. Standards work is community work of an unglamorous kind: the result is a document that thousands of implementers will use and few will cite, produced by consensus among national bodies over years. I have found it the most direct way of making formal methods available to people who are not logicians. The same commitment governs the proposed program: every axiom, mapping, audit result, corrected annotation and line of code will be released under an open licence and contributed to the COLORE repository, so that groups without access to proprietary robot data or to a verification laboratory can build on the work.

**What I would do at Princeton.** Four commitments, each specific. First, I would co-supervise [two] undergraduate independent-work or senior-thesis projects per year on the audit toolkit and the dataset studies [confirm the mechanism in the host's department], choosing projects that a student can complete and publish within a year. Second, I would run a short reading group, open across departments, on the theory of parts and wholes from Aristotle and Leśniewski to robot datasets — the program's subject is one on which philosophers, computer scientists and engineers each hold half the vocabulary, and the group is where they would meet. Third, I would present the audit tooling in a hands-on workshop for graduate students in the host's department and in [Princeton's AI initiative — name it once confirmed], aimed at students who have data to check but no formal-methods training. Fourth, I would represent the host laboratory in ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 and bring the committee's work — including the coming requirements on evidence for AI training data under the EU AI Act — back to the Princeton community in a form students can use. *(optional)* I would also take part fully in the fellows' cohort activities [confirm what the program expects], which are the audience I want for a program that argues a metaphysical theory should have engineering consequences.

The datasets my program will audit come from a small number of laboratories and depict objects, homes and workplaces from a narrow range of regions, and assume users with a narrow range of physical abilities. The ontology separates structural parthood from culturally specific object categories, and the audit will report coverage by source, so that these gaps are documented rather than hidden. That is the kind of contribution to the academic community I think formal methods are for: making explicit what a field has been assuming.
