**1. PhD research and past accomplishments.** I am a knowledge-representation researcher working at the boundary of formal ontology and machine learning for physical AI. I build formally verified first-order ontologies of physical objects and their parts, and I use them as specifications, audits and inductive biases for robot-learning datasets and manipulation policies. The question that organizes my work is one the Kempner Institute was founded to ask: what does an intelligent system, natural or artificial, actually represent, and how can we know? For one central kind of knowledge — how objects are made of parts — I hold that the question can be settled by proof rather than by analogy, and that doing so changes what machines learn.

**2. Fit at the interface of the Kempner research areas.** My proposal has three parts: a verified ontology of object parts, an audit of the datasets that physical-AI models are trained on, and training and evaluation methods that use the ontology as an inductive bias and compare what a policy learns with what people know. It sits at the interface of three things the Institute studies. With machine learning it shares the question of what structure a learned policy acquires and whether a symbolic constraint improves compositional generalization to unseen object categories. With cognitive science it shares the finding that parts are the primary units of human object representation, which the ontology turns into a formal account that both people and policies can be measured against. With the Institute's commitment to rigor it shares the standard that a claim about what a system represents should be a theorem or a counter-model, not an analogy. The first part I can do with the tools and collaborators I already have. The second and third I cannot do alone: they need faculty who train and evaluate learned policies at scale, cognitive scientists who study object representation, and the compute and engineering support to run policy experiments with ablations by axiom family rather than a single demonstration. The human-comparison study exists because of Kempner: it asks whether humans and ontology-trained policies disagree on the same objects, and whether their disagreements violate the same axioms, and that question can be posed only where cognitive scientists and machine-learning researchers share a building. The timing is set by the field: part-level datasets released over the past three years provide, for the first time, data at a scale where a formal theory of parts can be tested against what machines learn, and nobody has yet checked those datasets against any specification. I am already at Harvard, in a medical school laboratory; moving to Kempner is a change of intellectual community, not of address [confirm internal-candidate rules with the program office].
