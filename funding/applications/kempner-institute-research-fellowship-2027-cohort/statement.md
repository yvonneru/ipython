I am a knowledge-representation researcher who works at the boundary of formal ontology and machine learning for physical AI. I build formally verified first-order ontologies of physical objects and their parts, and I use them as specifications, audits and inductive biases for robot-learning datasets and manipulation policies. The question that organizes my work is one the Kempner Institute was founded to ask: what does an intelligent system, natural or artificial, actually represent, and how can we know? My answer is that for one central kind of knowledge — how objects are made of parts — the question can be settled by proof rather than by analogy, and that doing so changes what machines learn.

**Trajectory.** I completed a BASc in Industrial Engineering at the University of Toronto in 2015 and a PhD in Information Engineering in the Department of Mechanical and Industrial Engineering there, with all requirements completed in March 2025 [confirm date] under the Fast Track PhD program. My doctoral research developed an architecture for AI knowledge systems in which a verified formal ontology governs the data model, the learned models and the simulation layer; elements of that work contributed to ISO/IEC 21838-4:2023, the international standard for the TUpper top-level ontology, on which I was a core contributor within ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42. With Prof. Michael Grüninger I developed a theory of material constitution as a parthood-preserving mapping between mereologies, and a validation of mereological pluralism; both are under review at Synthese, and both give a machine-checkable answer to a question that robot datasets pose every day without asking it: when are two part decompositions of the same object compatible? **Why the Kempner Institute, and why now.** My proposal has three parts: a verified ontology of object parts, an audit of the datasets that physical-AI models are trained on, and training and evaluation methods that use the ontology as an inductive bias and compare what a policy learns with what people know. The first part I can do with the tools and collaborators I already have. The second and third I cannot do alone, and the Kempner Institute is the one place where the three things they need coexist: a community that studies representation in natural and artificial systems as a single problem, faculty who train and evaluate learned policies at scale, and the compute and engineering support to run policy experiments with ablations by axiom family rather than one demonstration. The human-comparison study in my proposal exists because of Kempner: it asks whether humans and ontology-trained policies disagree on the same objects, and whether their disagreements violate the same axioms, and that question can be posed only where cognitive scientists and machine-learning researchers share a building. The timing is set by the field. The part-level datasets released over the past three years provide, for the first time, data at a scale where a formal theory of parts can be tested against what machines learn; nobody has yet checked those datasets against any specification; and the fellowship's three-year term matches the sequence from specification to audit to learning experiments. I am already at Harvard, in a medical school laboratory; moving to Kempner is a change of intellectual community, not of address [confirm internal-candidate rules with the program office].
