## Auditing and aligning part-level annotations in robot datasets: a verified-ontology method for data quality and integration in physical AI

**1. Recent Progress.** My research axiomatizes ontologies of physical objects — computer-interpretable specifications of what terms mean — as theories in first-order logic and verifies them: every claim is a theorem or a counter-model. As a core contributor to ISO/IEC 21838-4:2023 (TUpper) [1], I worked on the axiomatization, modular organization and machine-checked verification of a top-level ontology adopted as an international standard. With Prof. Grüninger I developed a theory of material constitution as a parthood-preserving mapping between mereologies, and a validation of mereological pluralism, in two papers submitted to Synthese [2, 3]; the theory states exactly what must hold for two part decompositions of the same object to be compatible. My doctoral research developed an architecture for AI knowledge systems in which a verified ontology governs the data model, the learned models and the simulation layer [4]. Outside the university I have built ontology-governed data-integration systems that ran in production at two companies I co-founded, and I am currently a postdoctoral researcher at Harvard Medical School [confirm appointment title and dates; one sentence on the current work].

**2. Objectives.** Physical AI is being built on a handful of large part-level datasets — PartNet [5], PartNet-Mobility/SAPIEN [6], GAPartNet [7], PartNet-Ensembled [8], and real-robot corpora such as AgiBot World [9], Open X-Embodiment [10] and DROID [11] — that annotate objects as hierarchies of parts with kinematic, functional and visual labels. These datasets are combined freely in training pipelines, yet their annotation schemas were designed independently: a "part" is a segmentation label in one, a movable link in another, an actionable region in a third. There is no shared definition, no consistency requirement within a dataset (a part may be annotated as both contained in and disjoint from another), and no principled way to map labels across datasets. The consequences are visible in the field — poor transfer across part vocabularies, silent errors when datasets are pooled — but have never been measured, because there has been no formal specification against which to measure them. This is a data-quality and data-integration problem, and the proposed program is motivated by the following long-term challenge:

*Can the part-level annotations that physical-AI systems are trained on be shown to satisfy the axioms of parthood, can incompatible part vocabularies be aligned by proof rather than by convention, and does enforcing those axioms improve what models learn?*

To address this challenge, the program has three primary objectives:
1. **Ontology as specification** — axiomatize and verify a modular first-order ontology of physical object parts (rigid, articulated, functional, assembly) as an extension of TUpper integrated with the Process Specification Language (PSL) [12] for state change under manipulation.
2. **Ontology as audit** — a scalable method for checking hierarchical annotations against a formal specification, producing the first quantitative measurement of parthood consistency in robot datasets, provably meaning-preserving cross-dataset label mappings, and a merged, ontology-aligned corpus.
3. **Measured effect on learning** — a statistical evaluation of whether ontology-aligned data pools without loss and whether ontology constraints improve a part-aware manipulation policy's generalization to unseen object categories.

**3. Literature Review.** Data science has established that annotation errors are pervasive in benchmark datasets and destabilize model rankings [13], that datasets are rarely documented to the standard their downstream use demands [14], and that data work is undervalued relative to model work in high-stakes AI [15]. The tools that exist for these problems — label-noise estimation, datasheets, schema matching — treat labels as flat categories; none checks a hierarchy of labels against axioms that the hierarchy is supposed to satisfy. Formal mereology [16] supplies such axioms, and TUpper's mereotopology modules have been verified [1, 17], but no verified ontology distinguishes the rigid, articulated, functional and assembly senses of "part" that co-exist in robot data. [Prior ontological analysis of 3D vision benchmarks — cite the published paper(s) supplied by Prof. Grüninger — identified logical errors in ShapeNet and PartNet decompositions by hand, but produced neither a scalable audit nor a cross-dataset alignment.]

Neuro-symbolic methods that impose logical constraints on learned models [18] have not been applied to part structure in manipulation, and no evaluation protocol reports whether a policy's inferred object structure is consistent with any specification. The gap is therefore twofold: a specification that says what a consistent part hierarchy is, and a data-science method that measures existing data and models against it at scale. Relative to Prof. Grüninger's programs, which ask which mereotopologies existing decompositions presuppose, what is new here is the audit as a measurement instrument (sound bulk compilation, violation rates with quantified uncertainty, proof-backed mappings) and the statistical test of whether alignment changes what a policy learns.

**4. Methodology.** The ontology will be designed with the ontology lifecycle methodology of the Semantic Technologies Laboratory [19] and verified using the COLORE repository methodology [20]: we characterize the models of each module up to isomorphism and determine whether they are equivalent to the intended models, using Prover9 for theorems and Mace4 for counter-models [21]. Competency questions come from the data: every annotation pattern to be accepted or rejected is a sentence the ontology must entail or refute. Every artifact — axioms, adapters, mappings, corrected annotations, evaluation code — is versioned and released, so that each reported number is reproducible from the public inputs. Open research questions are milestones tagged by fellowship year (Y1, Y2); the Y1 questions form a complete 12-month program on their own.

**Theme 1: Specification (Objective 1).** *Which parthood relations are implicit in robot-learning annotations, and can they be axiomatized as verified extensions of TUpper?* **Project 1.1 — Parts ontology.** We axiomatize rigid parthood, articulated parthood (links and joints), functional parthood (affordance-bearing regions) and assembly parthood as separate modules in Common Logic [22], each verified for consistency and non-triviality, with representation theorems that characterize their models. **Project 1.2 — Constitution mappings.** We apply the parthood-preserving-mapping theory [2, 3] to fix when kinematic, functional and visual decompositions of one object are compatible, and integrate PSL so that parthood changes under manipulation (detaching a lid) are fluents. *Open questions:* Do the four parthood relations require distinct axiomatizations, or are some definable from others? (Y1) Is every dataset's part hierarchy a model of at least one module, and which axioms fail when it is not? (Y1)

**Theme 2: Audit (Objective 2).** *What is the rate and pattern of parthood-axiom violation in the datasets that physical-AI models are trained on, and which cross-dataset label mappings are meaning-preserving?* **Project 2.1 — Two-tier audit pipeline.** Adapters translate each dataset's annotation hierarchy into ontology instances; Datalog/SMT rules compiled from the axioms [23] check the bulk (millions of part instances), and residual hard cases go to first-order theorem proving; UNKNOWN and timeout are first-class outputs, and the audit reports coverage by source dataset, object category and region so that gaps are documented rather than hidden. Two properties make this a method rather than a script: the compilation is proved sound with respect to the axioms (every violation the bulk tier reports is a theorem of the ontology), and each violation rate is reported as an interval — confirmed violations as the lower bound, confirmed plus unresolved as the upper — tightened by proving a stratified random sample of the unresolved residue and reporting a confidence interval for its violation fraction. **Project 2.2 — Cross-dataset alignment.** We derive label mappings between datasets that are provably meaning-preserving under the constitution theory, correct violating annotations, and release a merged, ontology-aligned corpus and a reusable audit toolkit. The method is general: any hierarchical annotation scheme with an intended semantics (medical-image part labels, CAD assemblies, building information models) can be audited the same way. *Open questions:* Which fragment of the ontology compiles to SMT without losing violations that the prover finds? (Y1) Do violation rates differ systematically between simulated (PartNet-Mobility) and real-robot (AgiBot World, DROID) corpora, and between annotation pipelines? (Y1–Y2)

**Theme 3: Measured effect on learning (Objective 3).** *Does ontology alignment let data pool without loss, and does enforcing the parthood axioms improve a manipulation policy's generalization to unseen object categories?* **Project 3.1 — Pooling and constraint-based training.** We train part-aware manipulation policies on pooled ontology-aligned versus unaligned data, and implement differentiable relaxations of parthood constraints as auxiliary losses on inferred part relations [18], with constraint-guided augmentation for underrepresented categories. **Project 3.2 — Verification-in-the-loop evaluation.** We report, alongside task success, the rate at which a policy's inferred object structure violates the ontology. The evaluation is statistical by design: hypotheses, metrics and analysis fixed in a pre-registered plan before training; matched comparisons on held-out PartNet-Mobility categories in SAPIEN [6] and [second simulator — confirm]; bootstrap confidence intervals over [n — e.g., 5] seeds and repeated category splits; effect sizes with a multiple-comparison correction across axiom families. Policies start from the baselines released with SAPIEN and GAPartNet [6, 7], so no new learning architecture is required; co-supervisor [name] provides the policy-learning pipeline and the evaluation design. Fallback if that pipeline is unavailable: audit already-released policies for violation rate and its relation to task failure, which needs no training. *Open questions:* Does ontology-consistent training improve generalization to unseen categories? (Y2) Does violation rate predict task failure, and can it serve as an interpretable failure signal? (Y2) Do policies trained on pooled, ontology-aligned data transfer across part vocabularies? (Y2)

**5. Impact.** The program delivers a verified parts ontology under an open licence, contributed to COLORE and ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 32; the first quantitative audit of parthood consistency in robot datasets, with corrected annotations, cross-dataset mappings and a merged corpus released publicly; an audit toolkit packaged for reuse on other hierarchical annotation problems; and two publications — an audit/data paper at a leading AI or robotics venue and a methods paper on specification-based annotation auditing — with a third, on constraint-based training, if the fellowship runs to a second year.

In DSI's terms, Objective 2 is the development of novel methodology — a specification-based approach to data quality and integration for hierarchical annotations — and Objective 3 is the innovative application of existing statistical and neuro-symbolic approaches to a domain where the datasets are large, public and consequential. The project is interdisciplinary by construction (formal methods and robotics/statistics), reproducible by design (all axioms, mappings and code released), and it produces reusable tooling and data rather than a single result. For the fellow it is also training: the experimental design, uncertainty quantification and deep robot learning in Themes 2–3 are skills I will acquire from the second co-supervisor and the DSI community, and the audit method will be offered back to that community as a [workshop or tutorial — verify what DSI offers fellows].
