## Response 2 — Interest in the fellowship

I work on a specific trustworthy-AI problem that sits between disciplines. Robot manipulation policies are trained on datasets that annotate objects as hierarchies of parts: PartNet, PartNet-Mobility, GAPartNet, AgiBot World, Open X-Embodiment, DROID. Each dataset defines "part" operationally; none is required to satisfy the axioms of parthood; there is no principled way to map labels across datasets; and pooled training silently mixes incompatible vocabularies. The failures this causes are observed but have never been measured, because there has been no specification to measure against. My plan supplies one: a verified ontology of object parts as an extension of ISO/IEC 21838-4, a two-tier audit pipeline that produces the first quantitative measurement of parthood consistency in robot datasets, and an evaluation protocol that reports a policy's ontology-violation rate alongside task success as an interpretable failure signal. The evidence packs are structured so that they can be cited under the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and the EU AI Act's data-governance obligations, which reach robots under Annex I from August 2028.

I can build the technical half of this alone; I have done the theory, the standard and the production systems. What I cannot do alone is make the evidence mean something to the people who need it. Three questions in my plan are not computing questions. Who decides which decomposition of an object is "correct" when a dataset's annotators and my ontology disagree? What does a machine-checked evidence pack need to contain for a regulator, an assessment body or an affected community to treat it as accountability rather than as a technical artefact? And how should the annotation workers whose labour produced these datasets be involved in auditing them? Those are the questions I would bring to the Field School and to mentorship from scholars who work across computing, policy and society, and I would bring back, in return, a working verification method, a route into the ISO/IEC standards committee for ISO/IEC 21838 (JTC 1/SC 32) [confirm current participation], and a founder's understanding of how data companies actually behave.

I also want the cohort. My work has been carried out in a formal-methods laboratory, a medical school and two companies; I have not yet had a peer group that treats trustworthiness as the object of study rather than a property to be bolted on. A year with people whose primary training is in the social sciences, working on the same questions from the other side, is the most direct way to make my audits answerable to something beyond theorem provers.

## Response 3 — Community-based and public-interest approaches to trustworthy AI

My experience with public-interest technology has been practical rather than programmatic, and I want to be precise about it.

Standards as public infrastructure. I contributed to ISO/IEC 21838-4 within ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 32, the international committee responsible for data-management and interchange standards [confirm role wording]. Standards work is slow, consensus-based and unglamorous, and it is one of the few places where technical definitions are negotiated across countries and interests before products ship. I intend to offer the parts ontology from my plan to SC 32 and to deposit it in the open COLORE repository so that the specification is public property, not a company asset.

Openness by default. Every axiom, mapping, audit script and corrected annotation in my plan is released under an open licence, so that laboratories without proprietary robot data can build on the audit, and so that anyone can re-run the evidence rather than take my word for it.

Accountability to the people in the data. Robot datasets depict homes, workplaces and objects from a narrow range of regions, and they are annotated by workers whose guidelines are rarely published.
