## Background statement — Yi Ru

I work at the boundary between formal ontology and machine learning for physical AI. My interest is a single question that has a philosophical form and an engineering form. Philosophically: what does it take for two decompositions of the same object into parts to be compatible? In engineering terms: can the part-level representations that robot-learning systems acquire be shown to satisfy the axioms of parthood that humans use, and does enforcing those axioms make the systems generalize better? I have spent the last several years building the tools to ask that question precisely, and I am applying to the Klarman Fellowship because three years at Cornell is where I can answer it.

**How I got here.** I trained as an industrial engineer at the University of Toronto and returned there for a doctorate in Information Engineering, completed in March 2025 [confirm the date of conferral; see README], in the Department of Mechanical and Industrial Engineering. My thesis developed an architecture for AI knowledge systems in which a verified formal ontology governs the data model, the learned models and the simulation layer, and elements of it contributed to ISO/IEC 21838-4:2023, the international standard for the TUpper top-level ontology, on which I was a core contributor within ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 32 [confirm role wording]. That work taught me the standard I now hold every ontology to: every claim about it is a theorem or a counter-model, not a gloss. Alongside the thesis, with Michael Grüninger, I developed a formal theory of material constitution as a parthood-preserving mapping between mereologies and a validation of mereological pluralism, both now under review at *Synthese*. The account is not a philosophical proposal in the usual sense; it is a machine-checkable theory that a reasoning system can use, and the two decompositions it was built to reconcile — the statue and the clay — have exact counterparts in the kinematic, functional and visual part hierarchies that co-exist, unrelated, in every robot dataset.

**Between theory and practice.** I have also worked outside the laboratory. As a founding team member of MICAS and co-founder of YourTable Inc. and Uing Technologies, I designed and shipped production data-integration and recommendation systems whose data models were ontology-governed, led nine invention patent applications in 3D recognition and indoor modelling, and released a consumer spatial-computing application on the Apple Vision Pro. Since [month year] I have been a postdoctoral researcher at Harvard Medical School [laboratory], working on [one sentence]. Those years gave me a discipline that formal work alone does not: versioned axioms, regression tests on inferences, reproducible pipelines, and a strong sense that formal methods are judged by whether they run. They also convinced me that the datasets on which physical AI is being built have never been checked against any specification, and that this is measurable.

**The program.** The description of proposed research sets out three objectives: a verified ontology of physical object parts as an extension of TUpper with the Process Specification Language for change under manipulation; an automated audit of the part-level datasets robot-learning models are trained on — PartNet, PartNet-Mobility, GAPartNet, AgiBot World, Open X-Embodiment, DROID — producing the first quantitative measurement of parthood consistency in robot data and provably meaning-preserving cross-dataset mappings; and training and evaluation methods that use the ontology as an inductive bias, with ontology-violation rate reported alongside task success as an interpretable failure signal. The ontology, the audit results, the corrected annotations and the toolkit will all be released openly and contributed to COLORE and to ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 32.

**Why Cornell.** The program is interdisciplinary by necessity rather than by preference. It needs formal methods and automated reasoning, a robotics group that produces and consumes part-level data, and a philosophical community in which the theory of parts and wholes is a live subject. Cornell has all three in one college: [host name]'s group in Computer Science [one sentence on the host's work and platform — confirm], the department's tradition in logic and formal methods, and the Sage School of Philosophy [name of a metaphysics or logic faculty member willing to serve as informal second mentor — confirm]. The College of Arts & Sciences setting matters to me specifically: the fellowship's cohort of fellows across disciplines is the audience I want for a program that argues a metaphysical theory should have empirical consequences.

**What I would do with three years.** Year one builds the ontology and the audit pipeline and produces the first audit. Year two releases the aligned corpus and runs the learning experiments in simulation. Year three replicates on a real robot platform and carries the audit method to a second annotation domain — CAD assemblies or part labels in medical imaging — to show it is a method rather than a result. The longer term is a faculty position in knowledge representation for engineering systems; a program that is my own, complementary to but distinct from [my doctoral supervisor's — confirm name] and my postdoctoral supervisor's work, is the step that goal requires, and the Klarman Fellowship's independence and duration are what make the step possible.

**Mentoring and community.** I was Vice-President of the graduate students' association of my department, a departmental representative to the University of Toronto Graduate Student Union, and a steward for CUPE 3902; I have been a teaching assistant in engineering economics, database systems and knowledge modelling. At Cornell I would [co-supervise undergraduate researchers on the audit toolkit; present the work in the Klarman Fellows seminar and in both the CS and Philosophy colloquia — confirm what cohort activities the program expects].
