## Career statement — Yi Ru

I became a scientist by way of a question I could not answer with the tools I had. Trained as an industrial engineer at the University of Toronto, in the years after my first degree I built information systems for companies I helped found: a recommendation engine, then the customer, inventory and logistics systems of a direct-to-consumer business that reached more than a million customers in a year. Every one of those systems failed in the same way. The data model said one thing, the people who entered the data meant another, and the learned models trained on the result inherited the disagreement silently. I went back to graduate school to find out whether the meaning of the terms a system uses could be fixed precisely enough to be checked by a machine. The answer, in formal ontology, is yes: a theory in first-order logic has models, and one can prove which models it has. That answer became my doctorate, my contribution to an international standard, and the research program I propose to bring to Stanford.

**What I have done.** My doctoral research at the University of Toronto developed an architecture for AI knowledge systems in which a verified formal ontology governs the data model, the learned models and the simulation layer; elements of it contributed to ISO/IEC 21838-4:2023, the international standard for the TUpper top-level ontology, on which I was a core contributor within ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 32 [confirm role wording]. With Michael Grüninger I developed a formal theory of material constitution as a parthood-preserving mapping between distinct mereologies, and a validation of mereological pluralism — two papers now under review at *Synthese* — in which the philosophical puzzles of the statue and the clay become theorems and counter-models found by automated provers. In parallel I co-founded Uing Technologies, where we built structured 3D datasets of physical objects and indoor environments and shipped one of the first spatial applications on the Apple Vision Pro; I am first inventor on [number — reconcile] patents in 3D recognition and indoor modelling. Since [month year] I have been a postdoctoral researcher at Harvard Medical School, [laboratory], working on [one sentence — confirm]. That appointment has given me a second research environment and an applied community that judges formal methods by whether they work.

**What I want to do.** Robot-learning systems are now trained on datasets that describe objects as hierarchies of parts, and nothing requires those hierarchies to satisfy the axioms of parthood, to agree with one another, or to be respected by the policies trained on them. I want to supply the missing specification, measure for the first time how far the field's datasets depart from it, and test whether enforcing it makes robots generalize better. The theory exists in the two *Synthese* papers; the datasets released over the past three years supply, for the first time, data at a scale where the theory can be tested; and Stanford is where two of those datasets were built [in part — confirm]. My longer-term goal is a faculty position in knowledge representation for engineering systems, and the independence of this program — a research agenda that is my own, complementary to but distinct from my collaborators' — is the step that goal requires.

**Why a university-wide fellowship.** *(optional)* The program does not belong to one department. Its instrument is mathematical logic, its data are robotics corpora, its evaluation is machine learning, and its subject — what it is for one thing to be part of another — has been argued over since Aristotle. A cohort of [about ten] scientists from across the natural sciences and engineering is the audience I want, because a claim that a formal theory of parts should have engineering consequences is one that a chemist, a neuroscientist and a roboticist will each test differently, and the theory should survive all three.

**What I bring to the scientific community.** Three things, each with a record behind it. First, representing others: at Toronto I served as Vice-President of the graduate students' association of Mechanical and Industrial Engineering, as the department's representative to the Graduate Student Union, and as a steward for CUPE 3902, the teaching assistants' union — work that taught me how a university actually decides things and the habit of asking, in any new setting, who is not in the room. Second, teaching: I have been a teaching assistant for four engineering courses, from engineering economics to knowledge modelling, and have led teams of more than a hundred people in industry, most of them early in their careers; people do their best work when the standard is explicit, the feedback is specific and the credit is honest. Third, open standards and open tools: standards work is community work of an unglamorous kind, producing documents that thousands of implementers use and few cite, and it is the most direct way I know of making formal methods available to people who are not logicians. Every axiom, mapping, audit result and line of code in the proposed program will be released under an open licence and contributed to the COLORE repository.
