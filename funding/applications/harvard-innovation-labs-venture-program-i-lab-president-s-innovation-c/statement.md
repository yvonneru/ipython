## Founder statement (about 490 words; trim to the form's limit)

The thing that moves me is a gap that everyone in physical AI can see and nobody can measure.

Robots are being trained on data — teleoperated demonstrations, simulated scenes, world-model rollouts — and the data carries labels about the physical world: this is a part of that, this joint moves this way, this contact happened in this phase of the task. None of those labels is required to be true in any sense that can be checked. I know this because I helped write the standard that says what such labels should mean. As a core contributor to ISO/IEC 21838-4, the international standard for the TUpper top-level ontology, I worked on rules about parts, wholes, change and process that are machine-proven consistent [confirm role wording]. Then I looked at the datasets the field trains on — PartNet, PartNet-Mobility, GAPartNet, the new real-robot corpora — and found that each defines "part" in its own way, that a part can be labelled both inside and disjoint from another, and that pooled training mixes these vocabularies silently. With my collaborator Michael Grüninger I developed the theory that says exactly when two part decompositions of the same object are compatible; it is under review at Synthese. The theory is not the product. The product is the theory, run at scale, on real robot data, with the result packaged so that a customer, an auditor or a regulator can re-run it.

I have built companies before, and each taught me something this one needs. At MICAS I was on the founding team of a company that raised more than USD 5 million including Accel Partners, passed USD 50 million in revenue in its first year and grew to more than a hundred people; I built its CRM, ERP and warehouse systems and its machine-learning marketing, and learned that a data model that is wrong at the start is paid for every month afterwards. At Uing Technologies I led a team that built a 100,000-package library of measured 3D assets and one of the first AR pet-interaction applications on the Apple Vision Pro, and filed nine invention patent applications.

Harvard is where this company belongs for the next year for two reasons. The practical one: the buyers — humanoid and mobile-manipulation OEMs, medical-device makers, data platforms — are in Boston, and the i-lab's advisors and the HBS network are how a technical founder learns to sell certification rather than software. The scientific one: my postdoctoral work at Harvard Medical School [one sentence on the work — confirm] is the same problem in a different domain, and the audit toolkit we are building for robot data applies to part labels in medical imaging just as directly.

Physical AI will be certified — by August 2028 in Europe, and soon after everywhere else — and the certification will be either a bespoke consulting exercise per model or a reproducible artifact anyone can check. I want it to be the second.
