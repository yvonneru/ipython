## Founder statement (Dr. Yi Ru) — ~600 words; trim to the form limit

I have spent ten years on one question: how do you make a machine's representation of the physical world provable rather than plausible? As a doctoral student at the University of Toronto I worked on the axiomatization and machine-checked verification of TUpper, which became ISO/IEC 21838-4:2023, the first internationally standardized top-level ontology with a complete first-order axiomatization. With Prof. Michael Grüninger I then developed a formal theory of material constitution — when two decompositions of the same object into parts are compatible — whose consistency is established by theorem provers, not argument. That is the theory behind the AXIOMALITY kernel.

I also learned, twice, what it takes to ship. On the founding team of MICAS I helped raise over US$5 million from investors including Accel Partners, build a cross-border consumer platform past US$50 million in annual revenue within its first year, and lead a team of more than a hundred people while we built our own CRM, ERP and warehouse systems and a machine-learning marketing engine with a return above four to one. At Uing Technologies, which I co-founded in 2022, we built structured 3D datasets of real rooms and objects, ontology-based representations of the relationships between them, and one of the first AR pet-interaction apps on the Apple Vision Pro App Store. I do not present these as proof that AXIOMALITY will work; I present them as evidence that I know the difference between a result and a product.

Why this problem. Robots are about to be trained on data that nobody has checked. About half a million hours of real-robot data exist against an estimated ten million needed, every hour costs what the last one did, and the industry's answer — world models and simulators as data engines — multiplies the volume of data entering training with no record of what is physically or logically consistent and no per-record provenance. From 2 August 2028 the EU will require traceable, auditable training data for AI in robots, and the existing evidence forms are documents, log samples and one-off audits that no machine can check. I find this problem compelling because it is the one place where my two lives meet: a formal theory that says what a consistent scene must satisfy, and a data business that has to deliver a checkable answer to a customer by Friday. The data Passport — a per-release evidence pack in which every percentage carries its denominator, every check carries its versions, and every unknown is reported as an unknown — is the product I would have wanted at Uing.

What I am honest about. We have not yet had an independent customer validate the Engine; the internal test is planned for Q4 2026 [update]. First-order logic is not universally decidable, and we will never sell "no contradiction found" as a proof. Our own falsifiers are on a slide in the deck: simple rules may match the kernel on a customer's data; grounding may be consistent and wrong; augmentation may raise our score and not the robot's; runtime guardrails may fail latency acceptance and stay offline. We pre-register those comparisons and keep the negative results. The team is small [headcount] and I hold a research appointment [state the arrangement and the Toronto plan from spring 2027]; I will be at every CDL session, and the objectives I set with mentors will be the ones I run the company against.

What I want from CDL. Not capital first — objectives. I want mentors who will, every eight weeks, ask whether the wedge is real, whether the recurring commitment is signed, and whether the second robot took fewer hours than the first; and I want the Toronto robotics and AI investors who can size a round to an 18-month plan with observable gates.
