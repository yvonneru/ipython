## 3. Lay summary [confirm the word limit; typically 200–300 words]

Robots learn to handle objects from large collections of examples in which every object is broken down into parts: a cabinet has doors, a door has a handle. Each collection describes parts in its own way, and nothing checks that the descriptions make sense — that a part is not recorded as both inside and separate from another, or that "handle" in one collection means what it means in another. When collections are combined to train a robot, these inconsistencies are mixed in silently, and robots that perform well on familiar objects fail on new ones without anyone being able to say why. I will write down, in a precise mathematical language, what it means to be a part of an object, and prove with computer tools that the description is consistent. I will then use it to check the major robot-learning collections automatically, publish the first measurement of how consistent they actually are, and correct them. Finally, working with [host group], I will test whether robots trained on corrected, consistent data handle unfamiliar objects better, and whether the number of rules a robot's internal picture of an object breaks predicts when it will fail. The tools will be released openly. Beyond robotics they apply to any data organised as parts of wholes — engineering designs, buildings, and medical images — and they give regulators a checkable piece of evidence about the data that safety-critical AI systems are trained on.
